What QC Evidence Actually Proves

2026-11-05 XINQIU Engineering Dept BSCI Audited Factory

Almost every factory claims strict quality control. Very few can show a record that would let you reject a container. This is how to tell the difference before you place a container-level order.

Quality control is the hardest part of a factory to verify and the easiest part to describe. The test is not whether a supplier has a QC paragraph on their website, but whether they can hand you a document that would let you reject a shipment.

1. AQL Sampling and 100% Inspection Are Not Alternatives

Most third-party inspections work to an acceptance sampling scheme, where a defined number of cartons is drawn and a defect count above a threshold triggers rejection. Sampling is appropriate for high-volume, low-unit-value goods and for pre-shipment verification by a third party. It is not the same as inspecting every unit. For tents — where a single structural failure ruins a customer relationship — a documented 100% post-assembly inspection is the stronger position, provided you can see the record.

2. What an Inspection Record Should Contain

A usable record is a document with: the order and style reference; the date of inspection; the inspector's identity; the checklist actually used; the result per line item including pass/fail counts; and a disposition for anything failed. If any of those is missing, the document cannot support a rejection — it is a summary, not a record.

3. Photos Beat Certificates

A certificate tells you a management system was audited on one day. It tells you nothing about the unit in your container. Photographs and video of the actual production run — seam taping, pole fit, zipper function, packing — tell you much more. Ask for dated photos of your own order at each stage, not a stock library.

4. Five Questions That Expose a Paper-Only System

Who signs the record, and what is their role? Has a container ever been rejected at final inspection? What is the incoming material check for fabric? How do you trace a unit back to its batch? Can we send our own inspector? The answers separate a real quality system from marketing.

5. Separate Social Audit From Product Certification

amfori BSCI is a social monitoring programme, not a product certification. It evidences labour and management-system conditions, not that your tent is fit for a specific market. Product compliance for children's play tents, for example, is answered by EN 71 testing. State the distinction rather than trading on it.

6. Build the Checklist From the Specification, Not From a Template

The most common weakness in factory QC documentation is that the checklist is generic. A checklist that protects you is derived from the specification you wrote. When a specification has no corresponding line on the inspection record, that specification is not being inspected.

7. Splitting the Audit Into Three Stages

A single final inspection is the weakest possible arrangement. Buyers who audit well split the process: stage one incoming material, stage two in-process, stage three post-assembly. Most real problems become visible at incoming material, the cheapest place to catch them.

8. What to Do When the Record Shows a Problem

A factory that has never recorded a defect has a reporting problem, not a quality system. The useful question is not whether defects occur but what the disposition was. Acceptable: reworked to specification, replaced, or scrapped. Not acceptable: 'we sorted it out' with no record. Ask for the last three months of dispositions and read them for pattern.

9. Verification You Can Ask For at No Cost

Two requests cost the factory almost nothing and reveal a great deal. First, free fabric swatches so you can run your own tests. Second, a virtual factory tour by video call, walking the line during your order's production window. Both are standard for us, and a supplier who resists either is telling you something useful.
