Container Loading for Tents: How to Maximise Units per 40HQ

Two tents with the same FOB price can differ by 20% in landed cost per unit, because freight is charged per container and containers are charged per volume. Loading efficiency is part of the price.

Freight is billed by the container, not by the unit. That single fact makes carton engineering as important to your margin as the factory's FOB quotation — and it is the part of a quotation buyers examine last.

1. The Dimensional Frame You Are Loading Into

Series 1 freight containers have a uniform external width of 2,438 mm, with internal dimensions and ratings defined by the standard rather than by the shipping line. The practical consequence for tents is that a carton designed a few centimetres too wide can drop you from three rows across to two — a third of your capacity lost to a rounding decision. The authoritative reference is ISO 668:2020, which sets out external dimensions, internal dimensions for relevant types and minimum door opening sizes.

2. Ask for Four Numbers Before Comparing Price

1. Unit carton dimensions, in centimetres, for the packed product.
2. Master carton count — how many unit cartons per shipping carton, and the master carton dimensions.
3. Units per 40HQ, stated by the factory and, ideally, shown as a loading diagram rather than an estimate.
4. Whether the load is palletised. Palletising can cost 10–15% of usable volume but may be mandatory for your warehouse. If your factory quotes units per 40HQ without asking whether you take pallets, they are quoting floor-loaded numbers.

3. Tent-Specific Loading Wins

Compression matters more than folding. For pop-up and automatic-pole tents, bundle diameter — not length — usually sets units per container. A slightly smaller rolled diameter often gains more than a shorter package.
Pole separation. Poles shipped in a separate inner carton can nest into otherwise wasted voids alongside the canopy cartons. Ask whether the factory has considered splitting the pole set from the body.
Accessory nesting. Pegs, guy lines and carry bags can fill the gap between rolled bodies; a well-designed master carton uses that void rather than adding to it.

4. The Real Landed-Cost Comparison

Compare quotations on cost per unit landed, not FOB per unit. The formula is simple and unforgiving: divide the container freight and destination charges by the units actually loaded. A unit quoted 15% cheaper that loads 20% worse is more expensive. This is also why a supplier who can show a loading diagram is worth more than one quoting a lower unit price with no carton data — the diagram is a commitment you can hold them to.

6. Carton Design Decisions That Pay for Themselves

Carton dimensions are usually inherited from whatever box the factory already stocks, which is why so many tent programmes load below their real capacity. Four changes consistently repay the tooling cost. Re-cut to the container module. Working backwards from internal container dimensions, choose a carton size that divides evenly across the width rather than one that fits "nicely" on a pallet. Trade length for girth. Where a product can be rolled tighter, a shorter and slightly fatter master carton usually loads better than a long, thin one, because the dead space between rows shrinks. Keep master cartons uniform. A mixed set of carton sizes forces the loader to build a puzzle by hand, and hand-built loads rarely reach the volumes a diagram achieves. Design the void in. Accessories — pegs, guy lines, carry bags, repair patches — should nest in the gap between rolled bodies, not add to the master carton. Done properly, an accessory set can be absorbed at close to zero freight cost.

7. The Loading Diagram Is a Contractual Document

Ask for the loading plan as a diagram: rows, tiers and counts, with the units-per-container figure derived from it rather than asserted. This matters for two reasons. First, it converts a vague estimate into something the factory has committed to in writing — if the empty space in the diagram does not add up, you can see it before you order. Second, it gives you a benchmark. After the first shipment, compare the actual loaded quantity with the diagram; a recurring shortfall tells you the cartons coming off the line are not the cartons that were specified, which is itself a quality signal worth acting on. If the load is container-loading supervised, ask who supervises it and whether you will receive photographs of the loaded container before the doors close.

8. Palletisation, Staging and Season Timing

Decide on palletisation early, because it changes the carton design as well as the loading plan. Floor-loaded containers usually carry more units, but they take longer to unload and demand more labour at your end; palletised containers unload in a fraction of the time and protect the cartons, at a cost of usable volume. There is no universally correct answer — the wrong move is deciding it at the port. The same applies to staged deliveries. For programmes and retail seasons where goods must land in tranches, the loading plan should show how each tranche is built, not just the total. And where timing is tight, distance to the port becomes a scheduling asset: our Ningbo facility is about 30 minutes from Beilun Port, which shortens the loading window and reduces exposure to cut-off risk. The full sequence is described in how we work.

Get the carton dimensions before you get the price. Loading efficiency is a specification, and you can specify it.

9. Freight and Duty Are Not the Same Conversation

Loading efficiency decides how much you pay per unit in freight. It has no effect at all on the duty and tax you pay at destination, because those are assessed on the customs value of the goods rather than on the space they occupy. The two get conflated constantly, particularly when a buyer is comparing an FOB price from one supplier with a CIF or DDP price from another. The practical rule is to hold the commercial terms constant and compare loading separately: ask every supplier for units per 40HQ and per 40GP under the same Incoterm, then convert to cost per unit landed. Where a supplier offers DDP or CIF, ask which port and which inland leg are included, because a quote that stops at the port of discharge can look cheaper than a landed quote and be more expensive once the final mile is added.

For programmes shipping in tranches, the same discipline applies per tranche. A supplier who can show the loading plan split by delivery window is demonstrating that they have thought about your warehouse capacity and your season dates, not only about filling a container.

10. Three Mistakes Buyers Make When Loading Tents

The first is comparing FOB prices before comparing load factors, which makes a more efficient carton look like a more expensive product. The second is letting the carton be chosen last — packaging decided after the specification is locked is packaging designed around whatever box is already in the warehouse. The third is accepting an estimate as a target. Units per container should be a number the factory puts in writing with a diagram behind it, because that is the only version of the number you can hold anyone to when the first container is loaded. All three cost money quietly rather than visibly, which is why they persist.

Container reference: ISO 668:2020 — Series 1 freight containers: classification, dimensions and ratings.
